Every year, right around Thanksgiving, job seekers get another piece of advice they didn't ask for.

"Don't bother applying in December. Nobody hires during the holidays."

Really? Nobody?

After months of being told to tailor every résumé, network more, follow up, stay positive, and somehow stand out from the other 400 people applying for the same job, we're now supposed to just pack it up until January?

I'm sure the landlord will understand.

Look, there's probably some truth to the idea that hiring slows down over the holidays. People take vacations. Budgets get reviewed. Trying to schedule an interview between Christmas parties and year-end deadlines probably isn't anyone's idea of a good time.

But somewhere along the way, "hiring slows down" became "nobody hires."

And given how much terrible job-search advice gets recycled online, that seemed worth questioning.

So we went looking for actual numbers.

## Well, somebody forgot to tell the 3.9 million people who got hired.

According to estimates from the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_03132026.htm), American employers made approximately **3.9 million hires in December 2025**.

That's the non-seasonally-adjusted estimate. Not a motivational statistic from somebody selling a career coaching package.

Nearly four million hires. In December.

Now, before anyone gets too excited, that figure includes hiring across the entire economy. Seasonal workers, replacement hires, permanent positions, and everything in between. It doesn't mean four million people landed their dream careers.

And yes, the raw number was lower than November's. There really is a seasonal slowdown.

But there's a fairly substantial difference between fewer people getting hired and nobody getting hired.

In fact, when the BLS accounts for normal seasonal patterns, its December 2025 hiring estimate was actually a little higher than November's.

That doesn't mean December is secretly a fantastic month to find work. It means we should probably stop treating an ordinary seasonal slowdown like the entire economy has put up an out-of-office message.

## January isn't exactly the promised land, either.

Here's the part I found more interesting.

We've all heard the other half of this advice: just wait until January. New budgets, new positions, fresh hiring plans. Everything supposedly starts moving again.

And some of that is legitimate. Certain employers do start new hiring cycles with the calendar year.

But there's something else that happens in January.

**Everybody starts looking for work again.**

In January 2026, job searches on Indeed climbed as much as **31% above their early-December 2025 average**, according to [Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/02/19/february-2026-labor-market-update-new-year-same-resolutions/).

You'd think job postings might follow.

They didn't.

Overall postings stayed roughly flat. According to Indeed, they never even climbed above their early-December level during January.

More people searching. No comparable surge in available jobs.

That's worth thinking about the next time somebody confidently tells you to wait until January.

To be clear, this doesn't prove that applying in December gives you better odds. Job searches aren't the same as completed applications, and those numbers can't tell us what happened to individual applicants.

I'm not going to replace one questionable piece of career advice with another.

But the idea that January magically offers everybody a better shot? The evidence isn't nearly that convincing.

## Meanwhile, your application is probably sitting somewhere.

If you've been looking for work for a while, you know the routine.

You find a promising position. You research the company. You adjust your résumé, maybe write a cover letter, and then spend half an hour entering information that was already in the document you just uploaded.

Eventually, you hit Submit.

"Thank you for your interest!"

And that's the last thing you ever hear from them.

No interview. No rejection. No indication that an actual human being has even looked at your application.

Just an automated confirmation email and the slowly fading memory of what you applied for.

Now add the holidays to that experience.

Maybe the hiring manager is on vacation. Maybe the recruiter is waiting on budget approval. Maybe interviews won't resume until January.

Or maybe the company has already filled the position and nobody bothered to close the listing.

The problem is that, from the applicant's side, all of those situations can look exactly the same.

**Silence.**

And that's an important distinction. A company might still be accepting applications even if nobody is scheduling interviews this week. Your application could be sitting in a queue waiting for someone to return.

It could also be going absolutely nowhere.

The calendar doesn't tell you which one is happening.

Frankly, that's part of what makes this whole process so frustrating. Job seekers are expected to invest hours in applications while employers often provide almost no visibility into what happens afterward.

December didn't create that problem. It just gives everyone a convenient explanation for it.

## Not every employer operates on the same calendar.

Another thing that gets lost in the holiday hiring discussion is that we're talking about an enormous economy with employers that have very different needs.

A retailer hiring temporary holiday staff isn't operating on the same schedule as a hospital trying to fill a permanent position.

An engineering company waiting for a January budget decision isn't necessarily doing the same thing as a small business replacing someone who quit last Tuesday.

Some employers are winding down hiring. Others are actively recruiting. Some jobs are posted now with interviews planned for the new year.

And some of those listings probably shouldn't be online anymore.

That's why looking at the actual opportunity matters more than making assumptions about the month.

Is the job still open? Does it fit your background? Is the company actively accepting applications?

Those are questions worth answering.

"Is it December?" probably shouldn't be the deciding factor.

## So what should you actually do?

If you're hoping for some incredible holiday hiring trick that will put your résumé ahead of everyone else's, I've got bad news.

There isn't one. At least not one supported by the data we're looking at.

And if you've already spent months applying for jobs, I'm guessing another article telling you to "stay persistent" isn't going to change your life.

You know you need to keep looking. You've probably been doing little else.

What I'd suggest is a little more practical.

**Apply when there's something worth applying for.** If a legitimate position opens up in December and you're qualified for it, there's no good reason to ignore it solely because of the date.

**Don't confuse activity with progress.** Sending 40 applications to jobs you barely qualify for isn't necessarily better than submitting five well-considered ones. Especially if half the postings are stale and you aren't even sure the applications went through.

**Keep track of what you submitted.** Know which résumé you used, which positions you've applied to, and whether you received a confirmation. If a recruiter gets back to you three weeks later, you shouldn't have to play detective to figure out what they're talking about.

And please, **take a break when you need one.**

Not because some career expert announced that hiring season is over.

Because looking for work is exhausting.

Because getting ignored is exhausting.

Because watching your savings disappear while people suggest you just need to improve your LinkedIn headline is exhausting.

You don't need permission from the Bureau of Labor Statistics to enjoy Christmas with your family.

If anything, the numbers suggest you can afford to be a little more deliberate about when and where you spend your energy.

## The job market isn't closed. It's just the job market.

So, does nobody hire in December?

Of course not.

Hiring generally slows around the holidays, and some companies delay decisions until the new year. That's real.

But millions of people still get hired. Opportunities still exist. And January brings plenty of renewed job-search activity without necessarily bringing a flood of new openings.

None of this means you should spend the holidays glued to your computer.

It also doesn't mean you should pass up a good opportunity because somebody on LinkedIn said hiring is over until January.

If there's one thing the modern job market has taught us, it's that job seekers already have enough obstacles to deal with. We don't need to invent new ones.

That's a big part of why we're building [YourMove](https://yourmove.run). Applying for work involves enough judgment, uncertainty, and rejection without making people waste their time on the same repetitive nonsense over and over.

We're not particularly interested in helping people send a thousand applications just so they can say they sent a thousand applications.

We'd rather make the applications that matter easier to complete, review, and keep track of.

As for December?

Apply when it makes sense. Take a break when you need one. Get ready for January if that's where you want to focus.

Just don't let a piece of recycled career advice make the decision for you.

And the next time somebody says nobody hires in December, maybe ask them where they got that information.

I'd be curious to know, too.
